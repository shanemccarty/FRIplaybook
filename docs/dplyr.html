12  Transforming Your Data

How to use dplyr

Authors

Mara Estreich

Shane McCarty, PhD

Abstract

This chapter introduces students to dplyr, a core package within the tidyverse designed for efficient data manipulation and transformation. Students learn essential data cleaning techniques including recoding variables and creating new categorical and binary variables with mutate(), case_when(), if_else(), and factor(). The Play collapses seven education categories into three, cuts the ten-item K10 into its published bands, and combines the select-all RACIALIZED boxes into one variable. The Lab practices the same three moves on the lab dataset (EDUCATION_3CAT, POL_3CAT, HEALTH_3CAT, SES_01CAT), and Your Turn gives a template for the team dataset. By the end of this chapter, students will understand that clean, well-organized data is foundational for accurate analysis and will be equipped with core dplyr functions to transform raw survey data into analysis-ready datasets.

Keywords

data manipulation, dplyr, recoding variables, creating new variables, case_when

12.1 Introduction to dplyr

dplyr is part of the tidyverse and provides intuitive functions for manipulating data. Think of it as a toolkit for cleaning and preparing your data for analysis.

Why use dplyr?

  • Makes data cleaning code readable and logical

  • Uses the pipe operator (|>) to chain operations together, so that each step starts from the result of the one before

  • Essential for preparing data before creating visualizations or running analyses

Remember: You cannot create meaningful visualizations or run valid statistical analyses without clean data!

ResourcesResources: what is a .sav file, and why read_sav()?

Play 1 uses a dataset from an earlier FRI team that was saved in SPSS format, .sav. It is the same kind of thing as your team’s .xlsx file, a table with one row per person, in a different file type. The pattern from Import Data Once holds: the import line has the same three parts, and only the function changes with the file type. read_excel() (package readxl) reads .xlsx, read.csv() reads .csv, and read_sav() (package haven) reads .sav. user_na = FALSE tells read_sav() to bring SPSS’s “user missing” codes in as ordinary numbers, so that the next chunk can turn -99 and -50 into NA the same way you do for an Excel file. You will not need read_sav() for your own data unless your team receives an SPSS file.

12.2 📋 The Play

oralhealthdata <- read_sav("data/10.31.2024.oralhealth.sav", user_na = FALSE)
## replace with NA
oralhealthdata <- oralhealthdata |>
  replace_with_na_all(condition = ~.x %in% c(-99, -50))

12.2.1 Play 1: Collapse Seven Education Categories into Three (EDUCATION_3CAT)

When working with survey data, we often need to collapse detailed response categories into broader groups. This is useful for:

  • Simplifying analysis

  • Creating groups with adequate sample sizes

  • Making results easier to interpret

12.2.2 Original Education Variable

Our survey asked about education level with 7 response options:

  1. Less than high school

  2. High school diploma or GED

  3. Some college, but no degree

  4. Associate’s or technical degree

  5. Bachelor’s degree

  6. Master’s degree

  7. Doctoral or professional degree

12.2.3 Recoding into Three Categories

Let’s group these into three meaningful categories:

# Create a new 3-category education variable
oralhealthdata <- oralhealthdata |>
  mutate(
    EDUCATION_3CAT = factor(
      case_when(
        Education_Level %in% c(1, 2, 3, 4) ~ "Less than Bachelor's",
        Education_Level == 5 ~ "Bachelor's Degree",
        Education_Level %in% c(6, 7) ~ "Graduate Degree"
      ),
      levels = c("Less than Bachelor's", "Bachelor's Degree", "Graduate Degree")
    )
  )

# Check the recoding with a cross-tabulation
table(oralhealthdata$Education_Level, oralhealthdata$EDUCATION_3CAT, useNA = "ifany")
   
    Less than Bachelor's Bachelor's Degree Graduate Degree
  1                   11                 0               0
  2                   17                 0               0
  3                    8                 0               0

12.2.4 Play 2: Cut a Score into Categories (the K10)

Sometimes a published scale comes with cut-off scores, and a reader wants to know how many people fall in each band, not only the average. The lab dataset’s DISTRESS1 to DISTRESS10 are the ten items of the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; Kessler et al., 2002). The K10 is scored by adding the ten items, for a total from 10 to 50 (K10_TOTAL in lab_prep.R). Andrews and Slade (2001) proposed the cut-offs that most surveys use, including the Victorian Population Health Survey (2001):

K10 total Likelihood of a mental disorder
10 to 19 likely to be well
20 to 24 likely to have a mild disorder
25 to 29 likely to have a moderate disorder
30 to 50 likely to have a severe disorder

These are screening bands, not diagnoses: a score says how likely it is that someone would meet criteria in an interview, not that they do.

Plays 2 and 3 use the lab dataset, so this is the first time you run lab_prep.R. source("lab_prep.R") runs every line of that .R file, top to bottom, as if you had typed it: it imports ANTH306_LayConceptionsMH_SYNTHETIC.xlsx as mh_raw, keeps the serious and attentive responses, turns -99 and -50 into NA, reverses the _R items, and averages each multi-item measure into a composite (Creating Composites), all as mh_clean. From here on, every Lab starts with this one line instead of repeating those steps. The file must be in your project folder (next to your .Rproj, not inside data), and your load-library chunk must run first.

source("lab_prep.R")   # creates mh_clean, with K10_TOTAL

Open lab_prep.R and you will find DISTRESS_4CAT, DISTRESS_01CAT, and the RACIALIZED variables at the bottom, so that later chapters can use them. Plays 2 and 3 build them again in front of you so that you can see how. Running the code below simply replaces those columns with identical ones.

12.2.5 Four categories: DISTRESS_4CAT

case_when() reads its conditions from the top down and stops at the first one that is true, so each line only has to name its upper limit. factor() with levels = puts the four groups in order, so that tables and plots list them from Well to Severe instead of alphabetically.

mh_clean <- mh_clean |>
  mutate(
    DISTRESS_4CAT = factor(
      case_when(
        K10_TOTAL <= 19 ~ "Well",
        K10_TOTAL <= 24 ~ "Mild",
        K10_TOTAL <= 29 ~ "Moderate",
        K10_TOTAL >= 30 ~ "Severe"
      ),
      levels = c("Well", "Mild", "Moderate", "Severe")
    )
  )

# ALWAYS check a transformation: is every score in the right band?
mh_clean |>
  group_by(DISTRESS_4CAT) |>
  summarise(n = n(), lowest = min(K10_TOTAL), highest = max(K10_TOTAL))
# A tibble: 5 × 4
  DISTRESS_4CAT     n lowest highest
  <fct>         <int>  <dbl>   <dbl>
1 Well             91     12      19
2 Mild             50     20      24
3 Moderate         19     25      29
4 Severe            5     31      43
5 <NA>             23     NA      NA
#source: Andrews & Slade (2001) cut-offs; R4DS Ch. 3 (Wickham et al., 2023) https://r4ds.hadley.nz/data-transform.html
#explanation: K10_TOTAL is cut into the four published bands; the summary table proves that each band's lowest and highest scores fall inside its limits

12.2.6 Two categories: DISTRESS_01CAT

For a comparison of two groups (Compare Groups), or as a predictor in a regression (Relate 3+ Variables), a binary version is often all you need. The K10 cut-off for “likely to have a disorder” is 20 or more, so the line falls between 19 and 20 (a score of 19.5 is impossible, since the total is a whole number). A binary variable is coded 0 and 1 and named for the 1: here, 1 means likely distressed.

mh_clean <- mh_clean |>
  mutate(DISTRESS_01CAT = if_else(K10_TOTAL >= 20, 1, 0))

mh_clean |> count(DISTRESS_4CAT, DISTRESS_01CAT)    # Well -> 0; Mild, Moderate, Severe -> 1
# A tibble: 5 × 3
  DISTRESS_4CAT DISTRESS_01CAT     n
  <fct>                  <dbl> <int>
1 Well                       0    91
2 Mild                       1    50
3 Moderate                   1    19
4 Severe                     1     5
5 <NA>                      NA    23
#source: The Quantitative Playbook for R (McCarty, 2026)
#explanation: if_else() gives a 1 to everyone at or above the cut-off and a 0 to everyone below it; the count shows that the binary variable lines up with the four bands

12.2.7 Play 3: Combine Select-All Boxes into One Variable (RACIALIZED)

Teams asked the racial/ethnic identity question in one of two ways (Name and Recode Variables in Qualtrics). In the lab dataset it was select all that apply, so Steps 1 to 3 below combine the boxes; if your team asked it as single choice, skip to the shorter version after Step 3. Here, people could check more than one box, and Qualtrics exported one column per box: RACIALIZED_1 (American Indian or Alaska Native), RACIALIZED_2 (Asian), RACIALIZED_3 (Black or African American), RACIALIZED_4 (Hispanic or Latine), RACIALIZED_5 (Middle Eastern or North African), RACIALIZED_6 (Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander), RACIALIZED_7 (White), RACIALIZED_8 (Other, with the write-in in RACIALIZED_8_TEXT), and RACIALIZED_99 (Prefer not to say). Nine columns cannot go into a table or a model as they are. The plan: count the boxes each person checked, then use case_when() to sort people into groups, putting anyone who checked two or more boxes into their own group instead of forcing them into one.

12.2.8 Step 1: count the checked boxes

!is.na() turns each cell into TRUE (checked) or FALSE (blank), and rowSums() adds up the TRUEs across the eight identity columns. RACIALIZED_99 is left out: declining to answer is not an identity.

mh_clean <- mh_clean |>
  mutate(RACIALIZED_N = rowSums(!is.na(across(RACIALIZED_1:RACIALIZED_8))))

mh_clean |> count(RACIALIZED_N)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  RACIALIZED_N     n
         <dbl> <int>
1            0     3
2            1   166
3            2    19

12.2.9 Step 2: sort people into groups: RACIALIZED_6CAT

The first condition that is true wins, so “two or more” has to come first; otherwise a person who checked Asian and White would land in whichever single-box line came first. Groups with very few people (codes 1, 5, 6, and 8) are combined into “Another identity”, because a group of three cannot support a comparison. factor() with levels = puts White first, which makes it the comparison group in every model later.

mh_clean <- mh_clean |>
  mutate(
    RACIALIZED_6CAT = factor(
      case_when(
        RACIALIZED_N >= 2     ~ "Two or more",
        !is.na(RACIALIZED_7)  ~ "White",
        !is.na(RACIALIZED_2)  ~ "Asian",
        !is.na(RACIALIZED_3)  ~ "Black",
        !is.na(RACIALIZED_4)  ~ "Hispanic or Latine",
        RACIALIZED_N == 1     ~ "Another identity"      # codes 1, 5, 6, 8: too few to stand alone
      ),
      levels = c("White", "Asian", "Black", "Hispanic or Latine", "Another identity", "Two or more")
    )
  )

# ALWAYS check a transformation
mh_clean |> count(RACIALIZED_6CAT)
# A tibble: 7 × 2
  RACIALIZED_6CAT        n
  <fct>              <int>
1 White                 94
2 Asian                 19
3 Black                 31
4 Hispanic or Latine    17
5 Another identity       5
6 Two or more           19
7 <NA>                   3
#source: The Quantitative Playbook for R (McCarty, 2026); R4DS Ch. 3 https://r4ds.hadley.nz/data-transform.html
#explanation: people who checked two or more boxes become one group; single-box people are sorted by which box; the four smallest groups are combined; people who only declined get NA

People who checked only “Prefer not to say” match none of the conditions, so they get NA, which is what you want: a decline is missing, not a group.

12.2.10 Step 3: the binary version: RACIALIZED_01CAT

Many research questions compare people racialized as white with everyone else. Build the 0/1 version from RACIALIZED_6CAT, so the two variables can never disagree.

mh_clean <- mh_clean |>
  mutate(RACIALIZED_01CAT = case_when(RACIALIZED_6CAT == "White" ~ 0,
                                      !is.na(RACIALIZED_6CAT)    ~ 1))

12.2.11 The single-choice version: RACIALIZED_5CAT

If your survey asked for one answer, there are no boxes to count: you have one column, RACIALIZED, with the codes 1 to 8 (and NA where -99 was). Sort the codes into groups with case_when() in one step. Nobody can be in two groups, so there is no “Two or more”; that leaves five groups, and the naming rule says the name must say so: RACIALIZED_5CAT, not _6CAT.

alldata <- alldata |>
  mutate(
    RACIALIZED_5CAT = factor(
      case_when(
        RACIALIZED == 7              ~ "White",
        RACIALIZED == 2              ~ "Asian",
        RACIALIZED == 3              ~ "Black",
        RACIALIZED == 4              ~ "Hispanic or Latine",
        RACIALIZED %in% c(1, 5, 6, 8) ~ "Another identity"     # too few to stand alone
      ),
      levels = c("White", "Asian", "Black", "Hispanic or Latine", "Another identity")
    ),
    RACIALIZED_01CAT = case_when(RACIALIZED_5CAT == "White" ~ 0,
                                 !is.na(RACIALIZED_5CAT)    ~ 1)
  )

alldata |> count(RACIALIZED, RACIALIZED_5CAT, RACIALIZED_01CAT)   # ALWAYS check a transformation

#source: The Quantitative Playbook for R (McCarty, 2026)
#explanation: one code per person becomes one of five groups; the four smallest groups are combined; NA (declined) stays NA; the binary is built from the 5-group version so they can never disagree

Whichever version you have, look at the counts before you decide which groups to combine: “Another identity” exists because groups of three or five people cannot support a comparison, and your team’s numbers may fall differently.

CautionCaution: the name is RACIALIZED, and the binary is a teaching example

The variable is called RACIALIZED, not RACE, because the survey recorded how people are classified within a system of racial sorting, not a trait they carry (Fields & Fields, 2012; Zuberi & Bonilla-Silva, 2008; D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020; see Data Equity). When you report a result, write “people racialized as Black scored …”, not “Black people are …”. And RACIALIZED_01CAT is a coarse split that hides real differences between the groups it lumps together. It is here so that you can learn how a binary predictor works. A real report should also show RACIALIZED_6CAT (or RACIALIZED_5CAT).

GoGo: Name a new categorical variable for the number of groups it has

In this playbook, a variable made by cutting a score or collapsing categories ends in _#CAT, where # is the number of groups: DISTRESS_4CAT, EDUCATION_3CAT, HEALTH_3CAT, POL_3CAT, RACIALIZED_6CAT (or RACIALIZED_5CAT when the single-choice question gives five groups). A binary variable ends in _01CAT. It is either coded 0 and 1, or a factor with two levels, and either way the name says what the 1 (the second level) means: DISTRESS_01CAT (1 = likely distressed), RACIALIZED_01CAT (1 = racialized as a person of color, 0 = racialized as white). The name tells a reader what kind of variable it is before they look at a single value.

ResourcesTwo lab variables that would be better named: MH_TREATED and MH_KNOWS

The lab dataset has two yes/no variables, MH_TREATED (1 = has received counseling, therapy, or medication for a mental health concern, 0 = has not) and MH_KNOWS (1 = knows someone diagnosed with a mental illness, 0 = does not). They work as they are: they are coded 0 and 1, and the 1 means what the name says. But under the rules in Name and Recode Variables in Qualtrics they would be TREATED_01CAT and KNOWS_01CAT, and that is how you should name yours.

The ending does two jobs. CAT says the variable is categorical: the numbers are labels for groups, not amounts, so it should never be averaged or put on an x axis as if it were a score. The number in front says how many response options the variable has: 01 for two (coded 0 and 1), 3CAT for three, 4CAT for four. That number decides the analysis. A two-group variable goes into a two-group comparison or a binary predictor in a regression; a three-or-more-group variable goes into a three-plus-group comparison. The Analysis Map sorts the tests by exactly this count, so a name that carries the count makes the right row on the map easy to find, for you and for anyone reading your code.

ImportantRequired: cite the cut-offs

If you cut a published scale into categories in your report, cite where the cut-offs come from, the way the K10 references below do. Cut-offs you chose yourself are okay if there is existing theory to inform the decision or for practical purposes, but you must report what you did transparently in your methods section and with your R code.

12.3.1 Lab Play 1: Collapse categories (EDUCATION_3CAT)

EDUCATION has six levels (1 = Some high school or less … 6 = Graduate or professional degree) and a 7 that means Prefer not to say. Make EDUCATION_3CAT with the same three groups as the Play (less than a bachelor’s, bachelor’s, graduate), in that order. Then count EDUCATION by EDUCATION_3CAT.

Go Ref’s check: what should you find?
  • 90 less than bachelor’s, 63 bachelor’s, 31 graduate, and 4 NA.
  • The NAs are the people who answered 7. No line in your case_when() mentions 7, so they get NA, which is right: Prefer not to say is missing, not a fourth group. (This older survey coded it 7. Your survey codes it -99, which Import Data Once already turned into NA.)

12.3.4 Lab Play 4: Cut a score into a binary variable (SES_01CAT)

SOCIALSTATUS is the MacArthur ladder, 1 (bottom) to 10 (top). Its codebook row says to dichotomize at the top: 8 to 10 = higher subjective status. Make SES_01CAT, coded 0 and 1 and named for the 1, with if_else(), the way the Play made DISTRESS_01CAT. Then count SOCIALSTATUS by SES_01CAT.

Go Ref’s check: what should you find?
  • 41 people get a 1 (rungs 8, 9, 10) and 145 get a 0 (rungs 1 to 7); 2 are NA because they skipped the ladder.
  • In the count, every rung from 1 to 7 sits next to a 0 and every rung from 8 to 10 next to a 1. If rung 8 sits next to a 0, you wrote > 8 when you meant >= 8.
  • This cut-off comes from the codebook, not from a published paper, so your Methods sentence would say so: “subjective social status was dichotomized at rungs 8 to 10 for comparison with the rest of the ladder.”
