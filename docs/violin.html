19  Compare 1 Group, Pre/Post

The paired-samples t-test for normal data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for data that are not, with paired violin and raincloud plots

Authors

Erica Sava

Shane McCarty

Published

10.04.2026

Abstract

This chapter is the Analysis Map’s “compare 1 group, pre/post” row: the same people measured twice, before and after something, and one continuous outcome. Researchers learn why a pre/post design is a one-group comparison of differences rather than a two-group comparison, how to link each person’s two scores and keep only the matched people, how to draw a paired violin or raincloud plot that shows every person’s change, how to check the assumption that the differences are roughly normal and then choose the paired-samples t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and how to report each with an effect size. The Play uses the synthetic food security dataset from a cooking workshop; the Lab runs the same tests on the long-format lab dataset with Ref’s checks; Your Turn gives the template for Teams 3 and 5.

Keywords

paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank, pre/post, violin plot, raincloud plot, within-person

19.1 When is this the right test?

The same people answered the same questions twice: before and after a workshop, a video, a semester. On The Analysis Map that is the compare 1 group, pre/post row, and the test is the paired-samples t-test. The word paired is the whole idea: each person’s posttest score is paired with their own pretest score, so the thing being tested is the difference within each person, and the question is whether those differences average to something other than zero.

This is why it is a one-group comparison, not two. The pretest and posttest rows are not two groups of people; they are one group of people seen twice. Running an independent-samples test (Compare 2 Groups) on pre/post data is the most common wrong turn on the map: it throws away the pairing, and with it most of the power of the design. If you cannot link a person’s two rows, you cannot be on this row.

As in every box on the map, the shape of the outcome picks the test, but here the shape that matters is the shape of the differences:

Test Use it when R function Report
Paired-samples t-test (parametric) the within-person differences are roughly normal, or there are 30 or more matched people t.test(POST, PRE, paired = TRUE) M pre and post, t(df), p, Cohen’s dz
Wilcoxon signed-rank test (non-parametric) the differences are skewed, few people are matched, or the outcome is a single rating with a few levels or a count wilcox.test(POST, PRE, paired = TRUE) Mdn pre and post, V, p, r

Play 1 is the parametric case; Play 2 is the non-parametric case.

19.2 📋 The Play

ResourcesAbout the dataset: foodsecurity_SYNTHETIC.xlsx

In fall 2025 an FRI Public Health team studied cooking skills and food security among Binghamton students: a pretest survey, a hands-on cooking workshop, and the same survey two weeks later, linked by a self-made PASSWORD. The file here is synthetic, generated by a seeded script, so it can be public. It keeps the real survey’s variables and response scales, the pretest means, the share of -99 and -50 codes, and the long pre/post layout; the real export had only 45 responses and almost no one could be linked from pretest to posttest, so the synthetic file has 72 pretest and 60 posttest rows with 59 people matched, a few dropouts, one duplicate, and two mistyped passwords, which is exactly the mess Pre/Post Data teaches you to clean. Compare 2 Groups uses the pretest rows of the same file.

The research question is the team’s: did cooking self-efficacy change from before the workshop to after it? Two outcomes, one for each line of the box: food-safety self-efficacy (EFF_SAFE, the mean of four 1-to-6 items), a composite; and the number of five basic cooking skills a person says they know (COOKKNOW, 0 to 5), a count.

GoGo: reading a paired violin

The violin is a box plot and a density curve in one shape: wide where many people scored, narrow where few did. Two violins side by side compare the two distributions, which is what Compare 2 Groups needs. For a pre/post design the lines matter more than the violins: if most lines go up, the change is consistent across people; if lines cross in every direction, the average change is hiding a lot of individual variation, and the test’s p-value will say so. (Erica Sava)

Step 5: Check the assumption: are the differences normal?

The paired t-test does not care whether the pretest or the posttest scores are normal. It cares whether the differences are. Compute them and check.

food_matched <- food_matched |> mutate(EFF_SAFE_DIFF = EFF_SAFE_POST - EFF_SAFE_PRE)

food_matched |>
  summarise(n = n(), mean_diff = mean(EFF_SAFE_DIFF), sd_diff = sd(EFF_SAFE_DIFF),
            shapiro_p = shapiro.test(EFF_SAFE_DIFF)$p.value)
# A tibble: 1 × 4
      n mean_diff sd_diff shapiro_p
  <int>     <dbl>   <dbl>     <dbl>
1    59     0.431   0.743     0.593
#explanation: post minus pre, so a positive difference is an increase; Shapiro-Wilk on the differences, not on the two columns

The Shapiro-Wilk p-value is above .05 and there are 59 matched people, so the paired t-test is the right line of the box.

CautionCaution: paired = TRUE is not optional

t.test(POST, PRE) without paired = TRUE runs the independent-samples test from Compare 2 Groups, treats the same 59 people as 118 different ones, and usually gives a larger p-value. The output’s first line tells you which test ran: Paired t-test is what you want to see.

Step 7: Report it

A paired sentence reports both means, the n of matched people, t(df), p, dz, and the direction:

Food-safety self-efficacy increased from before the workshop (M = 4.51, SD = 0.87) to two weeks after it (M = 4.94, SD = 0.77), paired t(58) = 4.45, p < .001, dz = 0.58, a medium effect (n = 59 matched participants; Figure 1).

19.2.2 Play 2: cooking skills known, before and after (Wilcoxon signed-rank test)

COOKKNOW is a count from 0 to 5, so the differences can only be whole numbers, most of them 0 or 1. That is not a normal distribution and never will be, so this is the non-parametric line of the box. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test ranks the sizes of the differences, keeps their signs, and asks whether the positive differences outweigh the negative ones. It is the Mann-Whitney U’s paired cousin.

Step 1: Look at the differences

food_matched <- food_matched |> mutate(COOKKNOW_DIFF = COOKKNOW_POST - COOKKNOW_PRE)

food_matched |> count(COOKKNOW_DIFF)                                            # how many people changed by how much
# A tibble: 6 × 2
  COOKKNOW_DIFF     n
          <dbl> <int>
1            -2     1
2            -1     5
3             0    21
4             1    21
5             2     8
6             3     3
food_matched |>
  summarise(n = n(), median_pre = median(COOKKNOW_PRE), median_post = median(COOKKNOW_POST),
            shapiro_p = shapiro.test(COOKKNOW_DIFF)$p.value)
# A tibble: 1 × 4
      n median_pre median_post shapiro_p
  <int>      <dbl>       <dbl>     <dbl>
1    59          4           5  0.000641
#explanation: a count() of the differences is the best picture of a change in a count; Shapiro-Wilk far below .05 confirms what the count shows

Most people gained one skill, a third stayed the same, a few lost one. Medians, not means, are the summary to report.

Step 2: The Wilcoxon signed-rank test and its effect size

w_cook <- wilcox.test(food_matched$COOKKNOW_POST, food_matched$COOKKNOW_PRE, paired = TRUE)
w_cook

    Wilcoxon signed rank test with continuity correction

data:  food_matched$COOKKNOW_POST and food_matched$COOKKNOW_PRE
V = 642.5, p-value = 4.037e-05
alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0
# effect size r from the normal approximation, as in Compare 2 Groups
n_changed <- sum(food_matched$COOKKNOW_DIFF != 0)                 # people with a zero difference are dropped by the test
z_cook <- qnorm(w_cook$p.value / 2)
r_cook <- abs(z_cook) / sqrt(n_changed)
r_cook
[1] 0.665974
#source: R documentation ?wilcox.test; Fritz, Morris, & Richler (2012) for r
#explanation: paired = TRUE makes it the signed-rank test (statistic V), not the rank-sum test (W); r = |Z| / sqrt(n), using the n the test used; .1 small, .3 medium, .5 large

Two things to know about the output. With paired = TRUE the statistic is V, and R’s warnings about ties and zeroes are expected with a count: people whose difference is exactly 0 carry no information about direction, so the test sets them aside, which is why r uses the number of people who changed. And the first line of the output, Wilcoxon signed rank test, is how you confirm the paired version ran.

Step 3: Report it

The number of basic cooking skills participants said they knew increased from before the workshop (Mdn = 4 of 5) to after it (Mdn = 5), Wilcoxon signed-rank V = 642.5, p < .001, r = 0.67, a large effect; 32 of 59 matched participants gained at least one skill, 21 reported no change, and 6 reported fewer.

CautionCaution: a change is not an effect of the workshop

Both results say that scores went up between the two surveys. They do not say the workshop caused it: the same people might have gained confidence from two more weeks of cooking for themselves, or the people who came back may be the ones who liked the workshop. Write “increased from pretest to posttest”, not “the workshop improved”. A control group would be needed to say more, and your Discussion should say so.

mean(matched$DIFF) / sd(matched$DIFF)                                # d_z: .2 small, .5 medium, .8 large

# 4b. differences not normal, a count, or a single rating (non-parametric): Wilcoxon signed-rank and r
w <- wilcox.test(matched$SCORE_POST, matched$SCORE_PRE, paired = TRUE)
w
abs(qnorm(w$p.value / 2)) / sqrt(sum(matched$DIFF != 0))              # r: .1 small, .3 medium, .5 large
Criteria Ask yourself
Design Are these the same people measured twice, linked by PASSWORD? (Different people in two groups is Compare 2 Groups.)
Matched n Did you report how many started, how many finished, and how many were matched, and run the test on the matched people only?
Which test Did you check the shape of the differences and say which line of the Analysis Map box you are on: paired t-test (composite, normal differences) or Wilcoxon signed-rank (count, single rating, or skewed differences)?
Paired Does the output’s first line say Paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed rank test?
Effect size Did you report dz (paired t-test) or r (signed-rank), and say whether it is small, medium, or large?
Plot Does the plot show one line per person, and does the caption give the matched n?
Sentence Does your Results sentence have both means (or medians), the matched n, the statistic, p, the effect size, and say “increased” or “decreased from pretest to posttest”?
Claim Does Discussion say the change cannot be attributed to the program without a control group?
